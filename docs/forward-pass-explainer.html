Dagger Harmonics · A Walkthrough for Newcomers

From solar wind to sky map

DAGGER predicts a geomagnetic disturbance value at every ground station on the map from a short window of solar-wind readings. It does this in an unusual way: the network never predicts a station's value directly — it predicts a compact recipe, and a fixed piece of math turns that recipe into numbers at every station. Nine steps, each with the real numbers and shapes involved.

STEP 1

Input: a window of solar-wind history

The model reads a short stretch of recent OMNI satellite readings — solar-wind speed, density, the interplanetary magnetic field's Bz component, and more — one time step at a time. Think of it as "what has the sun been doing to Earth's magnetic environment for the last several hours."

STEP 2

A GRU compresses it into 8 numbers

A small recurrent network (GRU) reads the window one step at a time, updating a running "memory" as it goes. By the end, that memory — just 8 numbers — is a squished, learned summary of what kind of solar-wind conditions just happened. You can't read English out of them; it's a code the network invented during training, not a labeled dashboard.

STEP 3

A small FC head expands 8 numbers into 1,760

Two fully-connected layers (8→16 with ReLU and dropout, then 16→1760) turn that summary into 1,760 numbers. This step still has no idea where any station is — it only ever looks at the solar wind summary. Those 1,760 numbers split cleanly into four chunks of 440:

440 modes × 2 numbers (real + imaginary, like a clock hand needing both length and direction) × 2 fields (dbe, dbn) = 1,760. None of this is a location yet — it's a recipe.

STEP 4

What are the 440 "modes," really?

Spherical harmonics are the sphere's version of a Fourier series. On a flat line you build any wiggly signal out of sine/cosine waves at increasing frequency; on a sphere you need two numbers per wave instead of one, because a sphere has two directions (co-latitude and MLT).

l (degree, 1–20) is the zoom level — l=1 is one huge simple bump, l=20 is fine detail. m (order, −l…+l) controls how that pattern is oriented around in MLT. Summing (2l+1) over l=1…20 gives exactly 440 — 440 fixed shapes, from coarse to fine, that together can build up any disturbance pattern across the sky. (l=0, "the same value everywhere," is excluded — it can't describe something that varies.)

Each disc is centered on the pole (noon at top, midnight at bottom, dawn right, dusk left — same convention as this repo's station maps) out to the equator. The network never chooses these shapes; they're fixed. It only ever chooses how much of each one is present right now.

STEP 5

Each station gets its own "reading" of the shapes

A station's location — magnetic co-latitude and MLT — is plugged into that same fixed math to get 440 numbers: what does each shape look like exactly here? This is pure geometry. It doesn't know or care what the solar wind is doing.

STEP 6

Recipe × shape-here, summed up = one number

Multiply the model's "how much of shape k" number by "what shape k looks like at this station," for all 440 shapes, and add them up. That sum is the predicted dbe value at that station — not an approximation of it, the actual number the model outputs. Do the same sum again with the dbn coefficients for the predicted dbn value.

value(station) = Σk=1440 ( c_real[k] · Y_real(θ,φ)[k] − c_imag[k] · Y_imag(θ,φ)[k] )

STEP 7

Compare the reconstructed value to reality

Now — and only now — does truth enter the picture. The real measured dbe/dbn at every station with valid data is compared against what step 6 just produced, and averaged into one number: mean absolute error (L1). The model optimizes against these real physical nT values, never against the 440 coefficients directly.

STEP 8

The error flows backward — but not through the shapes

Step 6 is just multiply-and-add, so it's easy to differentiate. The error flows straight back through it into the 440-mode coefficients, then back through both FC layers, then into the GRU. Adam nudges every one of those weights slightly. The shapes from step 4/5 never change — they're fixed geometry, recomputed fresh from station coordinates every single pass.

STEP 9

Repeat — thousands of times, with a stopping rule

This whole sequence runs once per training record: shuffle, forward, loss, backward, step. Every epoch, a held-out validation set is scored the same way (no weight updates). If validation error stops improving for patience epochs in a row, training stops early and the best-scoring weights — not necessarily the last epoch's — are what get saved.

The whole point, restated: the network never learns "what the field looks like at Station X." It learns "how to translate solar-wind behavior into a spectral recipe." The map only appears when that recipe meets each station's fixed geometry — the same way a radio station never learns your specific speaker, it just broadcasts a waveform and lets physics do the rest.
